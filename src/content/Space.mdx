## The setting

Vrindavan is surrounded by green fields and native Indian trees, with views across the Anantagiri hills in nearly every direction. Sunrises, sunsets, and the night sky are all unobstructed. The air is clean — there are no vehicles inside the campus and over a hundred native species hold the soil — and the property happens to be oriented north-east, in line with traditional Vastu principles.

## The silent zone

The whole campus is kept as a no-pollution, silent zone. Vehicles aren't allowed inside, both to preserve air quality and to keep the natural soundscape intact. Parking for up to fifty cars is laid out just outside the silent zone, within easy walking distance of the main buildings. For luggage and for anyone who needs help walking, a battery-operated vehicle is available.

<Callout title="A note for visitors" variant="tip">
  Once you cross into the silent zone, you'll hear birds, wind, and leaves — and very little else. Most workshops take place inside this perimeter.
</Callout>

## Built spaces

The buildings on the property reflect the same values as the rest of the place — simplicity, sustainability, and a quiet relationship with the land.

### The mud house

At the heart of Vrindavan is a mud house, built from eco-friendly bricks made out of the property's own soil. Inside, a spacious main hall serves as the gathering space for workshops, meditation sessions, and group activities — naturally lit, naturally ventilated, and flexible enough for almost any layout. The kitchen is fully functional and supports community cooking during events. Three bedrooms accommodate small groups or visiting facilitators, with simple furnishings made from natural materials. The terrace above opens out to the sky and the surrounding greenery, and is a favourite spot for stargazing and slow conversations.

### The amphitheatre

An open amphitheatre sits within the campus, designed to work with the contours of the land rather than against them. The acoustics are natural, the venue is open-air, and seating is flexible enough to host anything from a small recital to a larger performance — music, dance, theatre, or a cultural talk.

### The zen garden

Adjacent to the main area, the zen garden is a quiet stretch of land shaded by a canopy of trees. Meandering paths wind through rocks and plants, with secluded corners for individual reflection. It's well-suited to meditation, slow walks, and simply watching the light shift through the branches.

### Open spaces for play

The rest of the property is open enough for children to run freely, without screens or supervision-by-the-minute — across lawns, between trees, around the rocks. The terrain is varied enough to be interesting, and contained enough to feel safe.

## A living botanical archive

Vrindavan is home to over a hundred species of trees and plants, chosen to celebrate the biodiversity of India.

<Callout title="A living sanctuary" variant="info">
  This isn't ornamental landscaping. It's a sanctuary of learning — where one can touch, smell, and recognise the plants that once filled traditional Indian homes, kitchens, and temples.
</Callout>

The sacred and heritage trees include the **banyan** (vata vriksha), a symbol of longevity and wisdom; the **neem**, a traditional purifier; the **peepal** (Ficus religiosa), considered sacred across India; the **bilva**, associated with Lord Shiva; the **paala maram** (Alstonia scholaris), revered in South Indian traditions; and the **kalpavriksha** (baobab), the mythical wish-fulfilling tree.

The flowering and shade trees mark the seasons — Pride of India in lilac during summer, gulmohar in fiery orange through the monsoon, the elegant sita ashoka, tall silver oaks, and weeping willows near water. The orchards include common Indian fruits such as mango (with rare Miyazaki varieties), jamun, guava, lychee, and sitaphal, alongside more unusual additions: avocado, passion fruit, surinam cherry, jackfruit, loquat, and coffee.

A small spice garden grows cinnamon, cardamom, nutmeg, clove, vanilla, and bush pepper — useful in the kitchen, but more importantly a living example of traditional knowledge. Larger tropical plants — banana, bamboo, alocasia, fiddle-leaf fig — provide cooling, privacy, and protection from the wind.

## Through the seasons

The land changes shape through the year. The **monsoon** (June to September) brings lush greenery, flowering trees in full bloom, and active birdlife. The **winter** (October to February) is the most popular season for workshops — clear, crisp air, comfortable temperatures, and excellent visibility for stargazing. The **summer** (March to May) is hottest in the middle of the day, but mornings and evenings are pleasant; mature trees provide shade, and many of the orchards are in fruit.

## Capacity and what's next

The campus can comfortably host between 100 and 150 people — enough for workshops, community events, training sessions, and family gatherings. Future plans include moving the campus fully to solar power, and adding a small gaushala for indigenous cattle breeds.
