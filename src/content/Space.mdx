## The setting

Vrindavan is surrounded by green fields and native trees at the foothills of Anantagiri, with views across the hills in nearly every direction. Sunrises, sunsets, and the night sky are all unobstructed. The air is clean — there are no vehicles inside the campus and more than a thousand plants hold the soil — and the land was oriented and planted as per Vastu principles.

## The land, and how it was treated

The 3.5 acres were treated artistically, aesthetically, and ecologically. Eco-friendly materials were used throughout — local laterite, granite, and basalt stones; soil for the mud architecture; minimal cement. The buildings were laid out as per Vastu, and key processes during construction were marked with devotional rites. More than a thousand plants of over a hundred native species were planted with intent — from simsupa and bilva to rudraksha, alongside fruit-bearing and unusual species like avocado and cacao.

## Components of the place

The land has been organised — gently — into a few named places, each with its own use.

### The Mattillu (mud house)

At the heart of Vrindavan is the **Mattillu** — literally, the house of mud (*matti* + *illu* in Telugu). It was built from the soil of the property itself, with eco-friendly bricks made on site; the pond is said to have given birth to it, since the soil and water for its construction came from there. Inside, a spacious main hall serves as the gathering space for workshops, meditation sessions, and group activities — naturally lit and naturally ventilated. The kitchen supports community cooking. Three bedrooms accommodate small groups or visiting facilitators. The terrace above opens out to sky and trees, and is a favourite spot for stargazing.

### The amphitheatre

An open amphitheatre sits within the campus, designed to work with the contours of the land rather than against them. The acoustics are natural, the venue is open-air, and seating is flexible enough to host anything from a small recital to a larger performance — music, dance, theatre, or a cultural talk.

### Residences

A small number of residences on the property provide simple, clean accommodation for visiting groups, facilitators, and overnight guests. The furnishings are deliberately understated — natural materials, quiet enough for restful sleep, with shared spaces that invite conversation.

### The zen garden

A quiet stretch of land shaded by a canopy of trees. Meandering paths wind through rocks and plants, with secluded corners for individual reflection. It's well-suited to meditation, slow walks, and simply watching the light shift through the branches.

### The tropical garden

A separate, lusher zone planted with larger tropical species — banana, bamboo, alocasia, fiddle-leaf fig, banyan and more — that cool the air, soften the wind, and give the garden a different character through the day.

### The pond

A natural-feeling pond on the property is one of the older fixtures of the campus. As the source of soil and water for the Mattillu, it has a quiet symbolic place in the design — and it stays a working part of the ecosystem, supporting plants and birds.

### The walking track and gardens

A walking path runs through the property, connecting the gardens, the buildings, and the quieter corners. It is short enough to walk easily, varied enough to be interesting, and made for the kind of slow, conversational walk that is half the point of being here.

## A living botanical archive

Vrindavan is home to over a thousand plants of more than a hundred native species, chosen to celebrate the biodiversity of India.

<Callout title="A living sanctuary" variant="info">
  This isn't ornamental landscaping. It's a sanctuary of learning — where one can touch, smell, and recognise the plants that once filled traditional Indian homes, kitchens, and temples.
</Callout>

<Carousel slides={imagesByCategory("plants", "tropical")} />

The sacred and heritage trees include the **banyan** (vata vriksha), a symbol of longevity and wisdom; the **neem**, a traditional purifier; the **peepal** (Ficus religiosa), considered sacred across India; the **bilva**, associated with Lord Shiva; the **rudraksha**, whose seeds are central to many Hindu traditions; the **simsupa**, mentioned in the Ramayana; and the **kalpavriksha** (baobab), the mythical wish-fulfilling tree.

Flowering and shade trees mark the seasons — Pride of India in lilac during summer, gulmohar in fiery orange through the monsoon, the elegant sita ashoka, tall silver oaks, and weeping willows near water. The orchards include common Indian fruits such as mango (with rare Miyazaki varieties), jamun, guava, lychee, and sitaphal, alongside unusual additions: avocado, passion fruit, surinam cherry, jackfruit, loquat, cacao, and coffee.

A small spice garden grows cinnamon, cardamom, nutmeg, clove, vanilla, and bush pepper — useful in the kitchen, but more importantly a living example of traditional knowledge.

## The silent zone

The whole campus is kept as a no-pollution, silent zone. Vehicles aren't allowed inside, both to preserve air quality and to keep the natural soundscape intact. Parking is laid out just outside the silent zone, within easy walking distance. For luggage and for anyone who needs help walking, a battery-operated vehicle is available.

<Callout title="A note for visitors" variant="tip">
  Once you cross into the silent zone, you'll hear birds, wind, and leaves — and very little else. Most workshops happen inside this perimeter.
</Callout>

## Current and upcoming facilities

The campus today supports workshops, retreats, team offsites, and small gatherings comfortably. **Currently in use:** the Mattillu and its main hall, the amphitheatre, residences for small groups, kitchen for community meals, the zen and tropical gardens, the pond, and the walking track. Capacity is comfortable for 100–150 people across the campus.

**Coming soon:** a dedicated reception block, additional meeting halls, a dormitory for larger groups, and a 300-seat indoor auditorium for talks, performances, and large workshops. The campus is also being prepared to move fully to solar power, with a small gaushala planned for indigenous cattle breeds.

## Through the seasons

The land changes shape through the year. The **monsoon** (June to September) brings lush greenery, flowering trees in full bloom, and active birdlife. The **winter** (October to February) is the most popular season for workshops — clear, crisp air, comfortable temperatures, and excellent visibility for stargazing. The **summer** (March to May) is hottest in the middle of the day, but mornings and evenings are pleasant; mature trees provide shade, and many of the orchards are in fruit.
